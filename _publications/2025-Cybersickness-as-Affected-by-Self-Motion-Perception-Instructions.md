# Project Summary

In my undergraduate honor’s thesis, I investigated whether virtual reality (VR) users could have their motion perception altered solely by instructions given to them, and whether this change in motion perception could affect VR users’ levels of cybersickness and spatial awareness. Participants were split into two groups and were asked to complete a simple navigational task, with both groups experiencing identical VR applications. However, participants were given differing motion perception instructions, with one group being told that they were moving around a stationary world (self-based motion perception), while the other group was told that they were stationary while the world moved around them (environment-based motion perception). Cybersickness was assessed using mid-session 0-10 ratings and pre/post-session CSQ-VR questionnaires. Spatial awareness was assessed using a post-session test where participants labeled the locations of various buildings in the scene, given a top-down overview. 

Participants in the environment-based group reported significantly stronger agreement with the idea that the environment was moving around them, suggesting that motion perception can be influenced by instructions. However, a low sample size of 10 participants was insufficient to identify statistically significant results in cybersickness and spatial awareness. Further research with larger sample sizes is recommended to assess the possible effects of differing motion perception.
